# Source Provenance Is Part of the Writing

Where did that sentence come from?

It is a simple question.

It becomes surprisingly difficult to answer once a manuscript has passed through months of research, drafting, rewriting, collaboration, and AI assistance.

That is why provenance should not be treated as cleanup.

It belongs inside the writing process.

## A claim without context becomes fragile

Imagine a sentence in chapter four:

“Most teams lose more time to coordination than execution.”

Where did that come from?

A report?

An interview?

The author's experience?

A remembered statistic?

An AI suggestion?

A sentence copied from an old note whose source was never recorded?

Those possibilities are not equivalent.

The sentence may still be useful.

But the editorial decision depends on knowing what kind of claim it is.

## Provenance means preserving the trail

For important material, preserve:

- source title
- author
- date
- URL or identifier
- page or section
- quotation status
- permission status
- notes
- what claim the source supports

For interviews:

- speaker
- date
- recording/transcript
- consent
- approved quotation
- context

For personal experience:

- identify it as recollection or interpretation where appropriate

The goal is not to make the manuscript look academic.

The goal is to make the manuscript reviewable.

## Why this saves time later

Writers sometimes avoid source tracking because it feels slower.

The cost arrives later.

During fact checking:

“Where did we get this?”

During legal review:

“Do we have permission?”

During editing:

“Is this still current?”

During revision:

“Did this statistic change?”

During translation:

“Is this quotation exact?”

During an updated edition:

“Which sections rely on this old report?”

A source-aware book answers those questions much faster.

## Provenance makes AI assistance safer

AI introduces a new source category:

**generated suggestion**

That category should not be confused with evidence.

If AI suggests a statistic, it still needs a source.

If AI summarizes an argument, the author should still verify the original.

If AI proposes a quotation, it must be checked against the actual text.

If AI rewrites a sourced paragraph, the source relationship should not disappear.

This is where good tooling matters.

The system should help authors keep the evidence attached even as the prose changes.

## Sources can disagree

Source tracking is not only about citations.

It also lets the author see disagreement.

Two reports may use different definitions.

Two experts may interpret the same event differently.

An older source may conflict with newer evidence.

A serious book should not hide those differences just to make the writing smoother.

Sometimes the uncertainty is part of the truth.

## Permissions belong in the same system

Provenance also connects to rights.

A photograph may have a source and a rights holder.

A long quotation may require review.

An interview may have consent conditions.

A table may have licensing terms.

A letter may belong to an archive.

If permissions live in a separate spreadsheet disconnected from the manuscript, it becomes easier to lose track.

## The reader may never see the whole provenance graph

That is fine.

The author and editorial team still benefit from having it.

Some sources become footnotes.

Some become endnotes.

Some only support internal verification.

Some material is removed before publication.

The system behind the book can be richer than the final page.

## A practical habit

Every time you add an important claim, do one of three things:

1. attach the source,
2. label it as personal interpretation/recollection,
3. mark it for verification.

Do not leave the category ambiguous.

That small habit can save enormous time later.
