# A Practical Book Needs a Difficulty Curve

A practical book is teaching something.

That means order matters.

Readers should not need a skill in chapter two that the book only explains in chapter seven.

This sounds obvious.

It happens constantly.

## Experts forget what beginners do not know

Once you have practiced something for years, prerequisite knowledge becomes invisible.

You compress steps.

You use terminology automatically.

You skip explanations.

The beginner cannot.

A practical book has to reconstruct the learning path.

## Think in dependencies

For every chapter, ask:

What does the reader need to know before this?

What new capability does this chapter create?

What becomes possible afterward?

That produces a difficulty curve.

## The first exercise matters

If the first practical task is too difficult, the reader may assume the whole method is beyond them.

Early actions should build confidence while teaching foundational skills.

Later chapters can increase complexity.

## Do not confuse simple with shallow

A clear early chapter can still be intellectually serious.

The goal is not to oversimplify.

The goal is to sequence complexity.

## Examples should increase in difficulty too

Start with clean examples.

Then add:

- exceptions
- ambiguity
- conflicting constraints
- edge cases

That teaches transfer.

## A workbook needs even more careful progression

Exercises should build on one another.

If every worksheet is isolated, the reader is collecting activities rather than developing capability.

## Test with a real beginner

Experts are poor judges of what is obvious to beginners.

Give the early chapter or exercise to someone in the target audience.

Watch where they hesitate.

Do not explain immediately.

That hesitation is data.
