# Metadata Is the Book’s Traveling Sales Representative

Before a reader opens your book, they may encounter its metadata.

Title.

Subtitle.

Description.

Author.

Categories.

Keywords.

Publication date.

Format.

Price.

Those fields travel farther than the manuscript.

That makes metadata one of the most important public representations of the book.

## Metadata answers the first questions

What is this?

Who is it for?

Why should I care?

Who wrote it?

What kind of book is it?

Where does it belong?

Good metadata does not manipulate those answers.

It makes them clear.

## The title and subtitle work together

The title can create identity.

The subtitle can create specificity.

A vague title may need a precise subtitle.

A highly descriptive title may need less explanation.

The pair should help the right reader recognize the book.

## The description should serve the reader

A weak description talks mostly about the author.

A strong description explains:

- the reader problem
- the book promise
- what the reader will find
- why the perspective matters

## Categories should be accurate

Choosing categories only because they appear easier to rank in can create mismatch.

The goal is to help systems and readers understand what the book actually is.

## Metadata needs consistency

If the subtitle differs across:

- retailer
- distributor
- website
- cover
- metadata feed

confusion follows.

Treat metadata as controlled product information.

## Metadata can change after publication

Descriptions, keywords, pricing, and categories may evolve.

Keep a record of what is current.
